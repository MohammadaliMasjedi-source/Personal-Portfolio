Sula Sweets · Logo round 3

Twelve references, four real directions.

Mo picked twelve Canva concepts. Stacked side by side they are not twelve ideas — they are four, repeated. This page pulls out the design system they all share, redraws every one of the twelve as clean vector, and tests each at the size that actually decides a logo: a 64 px avatar.

What all twelve agree on

This is the useful part. Individual concepts are opinions; what repeats across twelve of them is the brand. Every colour below was read off the references — none was invented here.

The thing nobody said out loud: those twelve Canva images are AI-generated pictures. They are flat raster art. There is no vector file behind them, no one-colour version, no transparent print file — and at the size of an Instagram avatar they turn to mush. They are excellent references and unusable logos.

Everything drawn below is real vector, so it can go on a box, a sticker, a 32 px favicon, or a one-colour foil stamp without redrawing.

The number on a stand

5 of 12 · the strongest family

A numeral is the hero, sitting on the navy pedestal. This is the only family where the logo tells you what the business actually sells — custom number cakes — before you read a single word.

Rosette number

The clearest of the twelve. The numeral swaps — 2, 7, 30, or a letter — so one logo covers the whole product line.

Survives small
Butter swirl + bold serif

Same mark, heavier voice. The serif reads more “patisserie” and less “home baker” — and stays legible on a small label.

Survives small
Sage line-art number

The most elegant and the most fragile. Those 3 px strokes vanish at avatar size and can drop out entirely in cheap printing.

Breaks small
Botanical wreath

Pretty, and the most generic — a wreath is the default move for every small food brand. Also the hardest to shrink: five sizes of detail at once.

Too busy
Mint number, overlapping script

The prettiest of the five. But the wordmark crosses the stand — overlap like that fails the moment anyone needs the mark alone, without text.

Won't separate

The whole cake

3 of 12

A finished cake or cupcake replaces the numeral. Warmer and softer — but it says “bakery”, not “custom number cakes”, which is the one thing that makes Sula different from every other home baker in Surrey.

Round layer cake

Lovely illustration, weak wordmark — pale teal script at that weight is the first thing to disappear on a printed box.

Weak wordmark
Naked cake + serif

The most confident wordmark of the twelve. But no stand, no numeral — it drops both of the things that make the family recognisable.

Off-system
Cupcake on stand

Best type lockup of all twelve — script name over a small serif line. Steal that lockup; the cupcake itself contradicts the subtitle under it.

Great lockup

The S monogram

2 of 12

The letter S built from cream or icing. This is the only family that gives a proper avatar — one shape, no text, readable at 32 px. It is also the only family with nothing cake-shaped in it.

Whipped-cream S

The best avatar here by a distance — one bold shape, holds at 32 px. Cream-on-white needs a defined edge or it disappears on a white box.

Best avatar
Pink S, side lockup

Reads well on a sticker. The pink S is closer to soap or cosmetics than to food, and side-by-side lockups crop badly on Instagram.

Off-flavour

The piping bag

2 of 12

The bag pipes the brand name itself — the maker's hand, mid-motion. Charming idea, and the hardest of the four to execute: it only works if the script it is writing is a real, licensed typeface.

Bag writes the name

Says “made by hand” instantly. But the name appears twice — once in icing, once in type — which is one time too many.

Says it twice
Two-weight flourish

Script over sans is a strong, modern lockup. Everything around it is decoration that will be cropped off the first time it is used.

Decoration-heavy

The 64-pixel test

Instagram, WhatsApp, Telegram, a favicon, a wax seal on a box — all of them show the logo at roughly this size. Everything that fails here fails in the place customers see the brand most.

What I would actually build

One system, three parts — not one picture

Primary mark: the rosette number on the navy stand (family 1). It is the only one of the twelve that names the product in the mark itself, and the numeral is a slot — 2, 7, 30, or the letter B — so a single logo covers every cake she sells.

Avatar & seal: the whipped-cream S (family 3). It is the only shape on this page that is still readable at 32 px. A full illustration cannot do that job, and forcing it to is why most small food brands end up with a blurry profile picture.

Type lockup: the one from the cupcake concept (family 2) — script name, then a small letterspaced serif line under it. That lockup is demonstrably the best of the twelve; it just needs to sit under the right mark.

The three share one palette, one navy, one berry set. Assembled that way, twelve references become a brand instead of twelve competing logos.

Before any of this can be printed

Four things are still open, and three of them are Soraya's call, not ours.

One honest note on the palette. Navy, sage and berry are not a new idea — they are almost exactly what Soraya wrote on her own intake form (white, blue, light green, no brown). Mo's twelve picks and her written brief landed in the same place independently. That agreement is the strongest signal in this whole file.
